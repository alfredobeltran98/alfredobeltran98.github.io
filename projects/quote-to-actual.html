Why does the invoice never match the quote?

A problem I was handed at a freight client, rebuilt here on synthetic data. They quoted one price, billed another, and could not explain the difference: the two figures lived in separate systems and the argument ran on anecdote. What follows is how I solved it, and why the interesting part was never the join.

Method

How the reconciliation works

The dashboard is the output. What matters is how a load gets classified, and which part of it I would still argue with.

01 / The join

Minute-level granularity, or nothing

first row per invoice ID, ordered by timestamp at minute precision

Quotes and final prices lived in different systems, but both carried the invoice ID, so the join itself was trivial. The problem sat on the billing side. Like most ERPs, theirs wrote a load in at zero and backfilled the calculated value afterwards, leaving several rows per invoice: one for the quote, one for each adjustment.

Picking the right row looked easy and was not. At day or hour granularity the rows tied, and a tie meant the ordering came back effectively at random, so the same query returned different numbers on different runs. Minute-level precision broke the ties and the result became reproducible. The lesson I kept: when a query is non-deterministic, the bug is almost never the logic, it is the sort key.

02 / The classification

Three causes, ranked by money

Once the numbers were reliable, the variance sorted into three buckets, and the order by value was not the one anyone expected.

A defaulted service charge, a liftgate in this case, sat in the account configuration and was priced into every quote whether the delivery needed it or not. That one inflated quotes rather than bills, so their customer was quoted high and billed fair.

Accessorials that only exist at delivery, residential addresses and after-hours windows, were absent from the quote and present on the invoice. Declared weight that did not match the load rounded out the list, usually a partial weight entered against what shipped as a full truckload.

03 / The instrument

A health metric, not a report

A reconciliation answers the question once, so I built a health check that answered it every week: what share of loads billed exactly as quoted, and for the rest, what caused the gap and which side it favoured.

That last column is the whole point. When an invoice got challenged, the argument stopped being two people asserting things at each other and became a number with a category attached, defensible line by line.

It is the same move as any good operational metric: pick one number that says whether things are fine, then make the exceptions explainable.

04 / Limitations

What I would push on

The cause taxonomy is a business rule, not a discovered one. Three buckets covered nearly everything I saw, but I never tested whether a fourth was hiding inside the accessorial bucket, and a category that absorbs too much is a category that has stopped being useful.

Attribution is also cruder than it looks. Calling a residential-delivery accessorial client-driven is defensible; saying the same of a weight mismatch assumes the quoting tool asked the right question in the first place. Before leaning on that split in a commercial conversation, I would want to know how often the tool itself invited the error.
