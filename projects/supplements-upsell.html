Case Study · Upsell Opportunity Ranker

What do I sell them next?

I worked at one of Mexico's top supplements companies through a stretch of growth the team couldn't keep up with. Reps were following up with every client the same way and guessing at who to call first. So I built them a ranking of where the money actually was.

How the ranking actually works

The table above is the output. The part worth explaining is how a client earns their place in it, and which parts of that logic I still don't trust.

01 / The calculation

Gap times momentum

Next Opportunity = Next Product Portfolio % × 12-Month Average of Monthly Volume
Total Opportunity = Portfolio Gap % × 12-Month Average of Monthly Volume

Every product line carries a typical share of a complete portfolio. Add up the shares of the lines a client already buys and you get their coverage. What's left over is the gap. Multiply by what the client actually spends in a month and the percentage stops being abstract and turns into pesos.

The two figures answer different questions, so only one of them belongs in the table. Next Opportunity is what a rep can act on this week, so it's what the ranking sorts on and the only number on the main view. Total Opportunity prices every line the client isn't buying, which is the ceiling on the account if it were fully developed. That one lives in the client panel, a click away, because it's a planning number rather than a call-list number. A client can be worth little this week and a lot over a year, and keeping them separate stops a rep from mistaking a small next step for a small account.

Why the twelve-month average. It's the most boring choice available and that's the point. Purchasing here is lumpy, so any window short enough to be responsive is also short enough to be fooled. Twelve months covers a full seasonal cycle and every restock rhythm inside it. The 6-month average and a 6-month EMA are both still shown on the client panel as context, they just don't set the number.

Which product gets recommended. The logic walks a fixed priority order of lines and stops at the first one the client isn't buying.

02 / What it replaced

Reps were working from instinct

Before this existed, the reflex was to push a little of something new at the biggest accounts, because those were the relationships that felt safest. It's a reasonable instinct. It's also exactly why the real openings stayed hidden.

A mid-size gym missing the flagship line is worth more than a large distributor missing protein bars, but nothing on a rep's screen said so. Volume and gap were never held up against each other, so the comparison never got made. Ranking by the two together forces it, and puts a number on the answer.

03 / Limitations

The cascade is still a heuristic

The order the recommendation walks was a business rule before it was anything else. The sequence came from margin and from how easy each line was to sell, which is a reasonable place to start and a bad place to stop.

So I checked it against internal transaction history rather than leaving it on assumption, using market basket analysis to read support, confidence and lift across the lines. That showed which products genuinely moved together and which pairings the rule had backwards, and the cascade got reordered where the data disagreed with it.

Worth naming the ceiling on that work. Lift tells you which products co-occur, not that recommending one causes the other to sell. Separating those two takes a designed test against a holdout group, not a better query, and that distinction is the part of this problem I find most worth chasing.

04 / About this demo

Small on purpose

This page is a working mockup running on 16 invented accounts, sized so it loads instantly and stays readable in a browser. The scenario it models covered more than 500 accounts across the Traditional Brick & Mortar sales team, which is where a ranking like this earns its keep. No rep can eyeball 500 rows and guess correctly.

Every number on this page is synthetic. Client names, volumes and product weights were all generated for this rebuild. No real client, employer or transaction is represented, and nothing here reflects the internal figures of any company I have worked for. The structure and the math are the real thing.

05 / A choice worth defending

Why this doesn't use an exponential moving average

An EMA weights recent months harder, and on paper that sounds like the smarter instrument: a client who is accelerating should outrank one coasting on old volume. I built it that way first. In practice it ranked the wrong people.

These clients don't buy smoothly. They load up. A gym or a pharmacy places one heavy order and then buys nothing for a month or two while they work through the stock, so a big purchase is often a signal that the next month will be quiet. Recency-weighting reads that spike as momentum and pushes the client up the ranking at exactly the moment a rep has the least to sell them. The rep calls, the client is still sitting on inventory, and the tool loses credibility on the first try.

A twelve-month average absorbs the whole restock cycle instead of chasing it. It answers the question the ranking is actually asking, which is how much this account is worth in a typical month, not how hot it looked last month. The EMA didn't get deleted though. It still drives the trend arrow next to each value, where comparing it against the twelve-month average is exactly the right use for it: good at telling you which way an account is moving, bad at telling you what it's worth.
